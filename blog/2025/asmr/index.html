Introduction

Training any classical statistical learning model, ranging from a simple regression to any fancy 2010’s boosting procedure, was deemed to be straightforward: make the model too simple, your out of sample fit would likely be very poor; make it too complex, your out of sample fit would also likely suffer. This tension - the bias-variance tradeoff - arises from the fact that more complex models will be able to better learn the underlying structure of the data, but will also be more prone to over-fitting idiosyncratic noise in the sample that comprises your training dataset, messing up your predictions on unseen data by confusing noise with signal.

During the past decade, however, for an instant, this piece of conventional wisdom was defied by the emergence of the “double-descent” curve, wherein we could observe that if we made models “complex” enough by expanding their number of parameters, well beyond the parameters needed for interpolation - where the model memorizes the dataset - we could still get very good results out of sample; perhaps even better than those of models that didn’t interpolate the data.

Since Belkin et al.’s (2019) seminal “Reconciling modern machine-learning practice and the classical bias–variance trade-off”, the field made great advances in understanding the phenomenon. The authors posited that over-parameterized neural networks develop “small norm” inductive biases as a consequence of the algorithms used to train them. In layman’s terms: even though on the surface a model with more parameters seems more complex, it turns out it is converging to an arguably simpler solution. If, rather than measuring complexity by number of parameters, we measure it by the Frobenius norm of the weights of the model, indeed models are simpler, and the double-descent curve disappears (Figure 2, Ng and Ma (2022)).

When the number of parameters is around the interpolation point, the model has just enough flexibility to contort itself exactly to the training data, but doesn’t have much freedom in what happens between the training data points. With extra capacity, the model can smoothly interpolate between the data points, and, in the absence of any extra information, these smooth interpolations are probably better generalizing functions than the highly contorted model that first interpolates the data (Figure 3). For my fellow econometricians, a great treatment of the topic can be found in Schaeffer (2023) .

Notice, however, we said the model can create smooth functions, but it’s not obvious why it would pick those out of the infinitely many possible solutions available when the number of parameters exceeds the number of training observations. Two factors seem crucial in the model picking smooth (low norm) functions: initialization and regularization.

Initializations with large norms have been found to generalize poorly in several contexts (Mehta et al., 2021 ; Xu et al., 2019 ; Zhang et al., 2020 ). Even more so, Liu et al. (2023) explain the phenomenon of grokking (a late double-descent in test loss, i.e., an improvement in generalization capabilities long after interpolation) from a similar lens. The authors find that large initializations can delay generalization because optimization takes time to reach a lower-norm region, while smaller initializations can remove that delay. Too small initializations, on the other hand, can cause vanishing gradients and won’t allow the model to learn. Fort and Scherlis (2019) found that Xavier and He initialization, widely used in the literature, land in a “Goldilocks” region associated with unusually favorable curvature and trainability.

However, the literature has mostly focused on initialization norms and not necessarily on developing new methods that explore different low-norm solutions in the loss landscape, mainly because Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) already imposes implicit regularization and explicit techniques such as weight decay (L2 norm regularization) are regarded as effective tools in this regard. However, due to the loss landscape being extremely non-convex, finding a minimum-norm solution is generally an intractable problem, especially so for deep networks. This greatly caught my curiosity.

In particular, a question that came to mind was in some ways the consequence of reframing what He initialization could be effectively achieving: if we rethink of He init as the smallest possible norm so that training is still “healthy” (no vanishing gradients) in the first pass, can we extend this framework to each step of the training algorithm? In other words, and breaking the question into two parts:

  1. Can we more aggressively (and adaptively) drift models towards a minimum norm solution at each training step, as compared to purely relying on SGD or standard regularization techniques? Think of at each step trying to reduce the norm of the weights as much as possible, subject to the model not collapsing (i.e., the pass’ gradient not vanishing).

  2. How do we know when to stop shrinking the norm? The most obvious thing to look at would be the standard deviation of activations. If we have shrunk too much, we would see representation collapse and should stop shrinking. Alternatively, the flip side of the coin would be some notion of dispersion in the gradients. In particular, we could use the gradient norm of the first layer in a Multi-layer Perceptron (MLP). If the gradient norm during backward passes goes to zero, we might have shrunk too much.

These two questions led me down a rabbit hole with some great, curious results.

Experiment Design

The first objective to pin down was which would be the target norm of the parameters we would like to shrink. The two obvious initial candidates were the Frobenius norm and the spectral norm. Continuing with the line of the literature discussed above, the former seemed like a more natural choice. They are greatly related, however, and controlling the Frobenius norm of a linear layer does indirectly control its Lipschitz constant (the spectral norm).

The second decision regarded which toy problem to start with. Because I was particularly interested in depth, and the health of the gradients traveling through the network, a teacher-student MLP problem with various depths served as a great choice. Moreover, depth is a crucial aspect of frontier models and their generalization capabilities, and the discussion in the papers above has mostly centered on shallow networks, reducing the depth problem to footnotes. I also intentionally avoid using any form of normalization (think layer or batch norm) so that the relationship between weight norm and model norm is as direct as possible. For depths 4, 8, 16 and 32, a random teacher is created, and an MLP of equal depth is trained over 2048 observations, validated on 2048 observations, and tested on another 4096 samples. Note that, intentionally, this will be a heavily over-parameterized problem.

The third and most crucial choice regarded our algorithm: how to shrink the norm, and what controller to use, thus answering questions 1) and 2) above. After a lot of tokens worth of discussion, in collaboration with Claude, the following idea came up.

  1. We will have as a target the radius of the hypersphere where the weights live. We initialize our target radius \(R\) as the total weight norm after He initialization, thus within the Goldilocks zone. At each step, if the network is still “healthy” (defined below), we will shrink that radius by a constant factor, and, if the current norm of the weights is larger than the radius, we will project / scale the weights down so that they satisfy the constraint that the weights live inside the radius \(R\).

  2. To evaluate whether the network is still “healthy” we look at the standard deviation of the deepest representation the model has built (the activations of the last hidden layer). We use the eval set for this and check on this at every eval checkpoint. When the SD has fallen below a preset fraction \(\gamma\) of its running peak, we stop shrinking.

Applying the radius layer-wise as opposed to the global radius produced the same results.

We call this toy algorithm ASMR (Annealed Shrinking-radius Max-norm Regularization), and we present it more formally in Algorithm 1 below. Note that ASMR is simply Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) with a time-varying feasible set - almost a trivial extension of standard PGD, and closely related to max-norm regularization, continuation methods and scheduled regularization, as discussed more in depth below. This works contribution lives in the resulting experiment: annealing a (non scale-invariant) model’s parameter radius without choosing a terminal norm in advance, following it all the way to collapse, and comparing the solutions it reaches with those obtained by standard regularizers. We have not found this particular training regime systematically characterized in prior work.

Algorithm 1 ASMR (Annealed Shrinking-radius Max-norm Regularization)

1:procedure ASMR(ρ shrink rate, γ trigger fraction, T steps)

2:initialize parameters w0 with He initialization and optimizer state q0

3:R ← ‖w0‖,  s⋆ ← 0,  shrinking ← true

4:best ← (w0, q0, R)

5:for t = 1 to T do

6:(wt, qt) ← OptimizerStep(wt−1, qt−1; next batch)

7:if shrinking then R ← ρR end if

8:if ‖wt‖ > R then

9:wt ← (R / ‖wt‖) wt  // project onto the norm ball

10:end if

11:st ← RepSD(wt),  s⋆ ← max(s⋆, st)

12:if validation improves then best ← (wt, qt, R) end if

13:if st < γs⋆ then

14:shrinking ← false  // collapse detected

15:(wt, qt, R) ← best  // model/optimizer rewind; keep data stream advancing

16:end if

17:end for

18:return wT

19:end procedure

Teacher-Student Toy MLP Results

The presentation of results will follow pretty closely the order in which they revealed themselves to me, because the discovery process itself was very insightful.

Figure 4 below shows the test losses of a very early run done using ASMR in the MLP teacher-student problem across different depths. The plot shows, in blue, vanilla Adam; in orange, AdamW with a weight decay coefficient of 2; and in green, ASMR with shrink rate 0.9995, and threshold fraction (of the running peak in SD of the last layer’s activations) \(\gamma = 0.1\), all with the same learning rate of 0.01. The weight decay coefficient for AdamW in this example was chosen so that it could reach solutions with a norm as low as ASMR’s ones. The results are intriguing. Two things immediately stand out: firstly, the regularization mechanism works, and it seems to be working pretty well; secondly, after some time training, the model is collapsing, so the controller is not being able to “save” it from collapse.

A couple of years as a researcher in financial markets is enough to instill an ungodly amount of skepticism about good initial results in even the most devout of people (like myself). So what followed was getting the controller straight ASAP, and throwing a battery of tests against it to get statistical significance.

The controller question turned out to be much harder than expected. Endogenously getting the model to re-converge after collapsing is hard - the optimizer, at least at the same learning rate, has a really hard time backtracking to its previous higher norm better solution. A plausible explanation for this is due to SGD’s implicit regularization. Importantly, as we can see from the initial set of runs, we definitely want to push the model to the very edge. Hence, the simplest solution was a “collapse-and-rewind” controller: push the model till collapse, and when reached, rewind to previous best val and keep training from there, now with a fixed norm. In Figure 5 we present the same depth 32 problem as above, but with the collapse-and-rewind controller. In the right hand side plot we can see the SD of the target representation, which quickly collapses right after the best solution under ASMR’s continuous shrinking pressure. Post-rewind, we let the model continue training at the fixed norm; though not much progress is typically done beyond that point.

With the controller question specified, the next step was to stress-test ASMR against tuned conventional baselines and ensure that its performance was not an artifact of a particular seed or hyperparameter choice. Note that there are two complementary, equally interesting, ways of comparing ASMR with standard optimizers / regularizers. First, given a particular problem (that is, a fixed teacher) we can tune each optimizer through a hyperparameter sweep specifically for that teacher, select the best setup based on a validation set, and then evaluate the resulting model on the test set. This mirrors the usual setting in which hyperparameters are tuned for a specific problem in hand. An alternative test is selecting one hyperparameter configuration using validation performance across several teachers and evaluating on a held-out teacher. This stricter protocol tests whether the hyperparameters transfer across problems of the same family.

So, for the first test: for ten different seeds, for each of the four depths {4, 8, 16, 32}, for each optimizer {AdamW, SGD, Adam+ASMR, SGD+ASMR}, we exhaustively grid-search over:

  • AdamW: learning rate, weight decay (16 configs),
  • SGD: learning rate, weight decay, momentum (18 configs),
  • Adam + ASMR: learning rate, shrink rate (16 configs),
  • SGD + ASMR: learning rate, shrink rate, momentum (18 configs).

For each (method, depth, seed) triple across configurations we select the best hyperparameters based on the validation set, and evaluate that configuration on a held-out test set. To keep Claude (and Casella & Berger) happy, everything is paired at each different seed (the teacher’s weights, the data, the student’s initialization and the batch order), so we make the most of each run in terms of statistical power. Analysis

Let’s start with the elephant in the room. Why let the models collapse? They are collapsing because we are shrinking the radius too much, eventually driving the models’ output scale below what is needed to make good predictions. We could very easily increase the representation standard deviation threshold at which we stop shrinking, which would immediately fix it. Why not do it? The answer lies in the most interesting feature of ASMR, at least in this “clean” teacher-student toy problem. The models are able to learn right until the very edge of collapse. Even more so, the models make the most improvement in their generalization capabilities right before chaos! This peculiarity is unwavering across different base optimizers (Adam vs SGD), learning rates, batch sizes, momentum, etc. And it is beautiful: the models consistently squeeze the most performance right in the last inning. There is no plateauing period, no stable loss before things worsen. The models become significantly better at generalizing, and collapse right after that.

To be more precise, we can quantify this edge across the selected 73 ASMR runs that collapsed within the training budget. Firstly, in terms of steps, the median time to val-best occurred at step 20,544, while the median interval from val-best to collapse was 3824. The within-run median of this convergence / edge ratio was 4.52x, so reaching val-best typically took more than four times as long than going from val-best to collapse.

More interestingly, let’s look at the behavior of the model’s generalization capabilities around the best solution. If we look at the last one-fifth window of steps before val-best (a median of 3568 steps), test MSE fell on average by 16%, compared to only 6.8% during the immediately preceding fifth. During the final tenth before val-best (median of 1912 steps), test MSE fell by 13.2%, compared with only 3.0% during the preceding tenth. Only in the last 16 steps before val-best, test loss falls 2.77%! So there is an acceleration in test MSE improvement as the model approaches the best low-norm solution. What happens immediately afterwards is equally striking. Only in the next 16 steps after val-best, test loss increases on average 3.00% and after 512 steps, by 6.38%. Among runs that remain observable for a complete symmetric tenth, it rises by 19.85%; 25 additional runs collapse before that window can finish. Over a complete symmetric fifth, test MSE rises by 29.15%, while 36 additional runs collapse before the window ends. In summary, the best generalizing model solution sits at a sharply localized turning point. Generalization improves at an accelerating rate as the optimizer approaches it, and deteriorates immediately after it passes that point. Table 6 summarizes these results.

Figure 7 deepens into the internal mechanisms of ASMR for the depth 4 example in Figure 4, where we let the model collapse and not rescue it. Plot a) shows the test and train loss (notice the log scale). This plot is extremely interesting. Very early on, the model interpolates the data. After the interpolation point, ASMR makes progress in its generalization capabilities, but at a slow rate, comparable to how vanilla SGD or Adam would do. However, at some point, the norm ball really starts to bite, and in fact starts increasing the train loss, corresponding to regularization getting tigher. In the edge of collapse, the model improves its out of sample significantly, and, steps after that, collapses. Notice that the final strong improvement in test capabilities occurs while the train loss is increasing!

Plot b) shows that, in this case, the norm ball is always biting, so at each step we are imposing a constraint over the solution. Plot c) displays the gradient signal at the first layer. As it is natural, when the model makes its initial steps and learns the basic structure of the data, the gradient signal in the first layer falls. But when the norm ball restriction becomes tight, the gradient starts to increase again - probably, in part, driven by a higher error in the training data, reflected in the higher train loss. And this higher gradient signal is indeed helping the model make real improvements out of sample! Importantly, this plot also shows why the initial idea of the (unnormalized) first layer gradient as a signal is not good. The gradient falls initially as training progresses, but then raises again while the model is still improving out of sample. This non-monotonicity makes any possible threshold based on the gradient a bad idea: too high, it impedes the latter improvement in the model, too low, the model collapses. Finally, plot d) shows the model’s collapse from the lens of its representation standard deviation. Combined with plot c), these two figures show a very interesting phenomenon: in those last innings where the model makes strong out-of-sample progress, the first layer gradient norm increases, and the representation SD increases too, despite an ever increasing pressure from ASMR on the model’s norm!

One could hypothesize that ASMR, by getting near the edge of collapse, is reaching lower norm solutions than the baseline optimizers and, as a result, generalizing better. Importantly, this doesn’t seem to be the case. Table 7 shows the mean and SD of solution norms at each depth; which are essentially the same between ASMR and AdamW. And at these matched final norms, ASMR gets up to 5% lower test loss in the problem-specific hyperparameter selection sweep. So ASMR’s trajectory seems to more consistently find better generalizing solutions.

Trajectory Comparison

So, how are trajectories differing, and why may ASMR reach better solutions? At each step, AdamW updates according to:

\[w_{t+1} = (1 - \eta\lambda) w_t - \eta d_t\]

where \(\eta\) is the learning rate, \(\lambda\) is the weight decay coefficient and \(d_t\) is Adam’s adaptive gradient direction. In contrast, in a step where the norm ball is binding, ASMR sets:

\[w_{t+1} = \frac{R_t}{\lVert w_t - \eta d_t \rVert}\left(w_t - \eta d_t \right)\]

Note that AdamW imposes a predetermined radial pull on the previous iterate, \(-\eta \lambda w_t\), where \(\eta \lambda\) is a scalar and constant throughout training (when the learning rate is constant). ASMR, instead, takes the optimizer’s proposed step and then projects the resulting iterate onto the prescribed ball. The size of this projection depends on both the current weights and the gradient that was just taken. If the gradient step grew the norm a lot, then the projection to the norm ball implies a higher contraction. This is important because it creates an automatic normalization of the contributions of each step to the norm trajectory. Under AdamW, the norm trajectory is a stochastic outcome of the balance between gradients potentially pushing the norm up, and weight decay pushing it down. Under ASMR, the norm is deterministic and exactly \(R(t)\), so the magnitude of regularization follows a prescribed schedule rather than emerging from the optimization noise.

But why may ASMR’s trajectories reach better solutions? This is a promising direction for future research, and some hypotheses are explored here. One explanation concerns where the regularizer acts. The standard L2 regularization’s penalty modifies the loss landscape itself. The penalty in the loss function $L(w) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert w \rVert^{2}$ deforms the whole landscape of \(L\), creating new minima and destroying others. ASMR keeps the loss intact, and just restricts the search to a sphere whose radius gradually decreases. Naturally, the radial projection will produce a net move in the weights that isn’t purely along the gradient of the loss, so it bends the trajectory too. However, the regularizer acts through the feasible set rather than by adding force to the gradient. In convex problems, the penalized and constrained formulations are dual (by strong duality) and reach identical optima, so this distinction is invisible. However, in the non-convex case, the descent directions differ pointwise, and since the path that the optimizer takes determines which local minimum is reached, the two methods can reach different solutions, even at matched final norm.

The hypothesis above distinguishes AdamW from ASMR by their instantaneous mechanisms (force versus projection). Another related explanation deals with the temporal mechanism: because AdamW applies a fixed shrinkage at each step, the model is subject to the decay force from initialization onward, including the early phase when it has not yet learned useful features. ASMR, in turn, reframes regularization as a homotopy on the constraint set. The radius \(R(t)\) begins at the He-init norm, where the model is healthy and learning is unobstructed, and then shrinks gradually over training. At every step, the previous iterate is a feasible (or near-feasible) starting point for the next, slightly tighter constraint. Thus, the model walks down through a sequence of working solutions, each a small perturbation of the last. We can then think of ASMR as a continuation strategy: solving a hard problem by continuously deforming an easy problem into it, warm-starting each step! The hard problem, “finding a low-norm model that fits the data”, is harder to reach from a cold start, as the fixed-radius max-norm controls show, but may be better reachable via ASMR’s path. So “curriculum learning”; but not over the data, as it is typically thought of, but rather over the constraint!

A final important hypothesis to consider relates to effective learning rates (ELRs). Recent work studies scale-invariant networks directly on the sphere and finds that the learning-rate regime can materially affect both dynamics and generalization (Kodryan et al., 2022; Lobacheva et al., 2023). Because ASMR decreases the parameter radius while holding the learning rate constant, it thus increases the optimizer’s relative angular movement in the parameter space (the same \(\eta\) goes a longer way for a smaller \(\lVert w \rVert\)). And because ASMR’s shrinkage is progressive throughout training, this increase happens progressively, creating a form of scheduled ELR increase. This connects ASMR to work on effective learning rates and optimization on the sphere. However, the networks being used in the toy problem aren’t scale-invariant (unnormalized ReLU MLPs are positively homogeneous), so their radius directly affects the represented function. Moreover, further diagnostics show that the rise in the raw gradient near collapse (Figure 7, plot c) is predominantly radial rather than tangential, and that 75%-80% of the deterioration post-best step is explained by lost output amplitude, rather than a chaotic equilibrium or divergence caused by an excessively large ELR. In summary, an increasing angular step may also contribute to ASMR’s beneficial trajectory, and doing so progressively seems to be important too (as compared to the fixed-radius max-norm baseline, which allows for that higher ELR from the start), but does not explain the dominant mechanism in collapse. Fully disentangling the effects of the shrinking radius and the induced ELR dynamics remains an interesting direction for future research.

Depth Effects

An important characteristic of bias-free depth-\(L\) ReLU MLPs is that they are homogeneous of degree \(L\), and so depth can significantly change how a given network may respond to a change in its radius. Let $W$ collect all the weights of a network in the toy example, and factorize it into its magnitude and its direction:

\[W = Rv,\qquad R=\lVert W\rVert_2,\qquad \lVert v\rVert_2=1.\]

Then, because ReLU satisfies \(ReLU(cz) = cReLU(z)\) for \(c > 0\), each layer contributes one factor of \(R\) to the output, and:

\[f_W(x) = f_{Rv}(x) = R^L f_v(x)\]

Then, under a radius contraction of \(\rho\), \(R_t = \rho R_{t-1}\), the functional scaling is \(\rho^L\). So for a shrink rate of \(\rho = 0.9995\), the effective functional scaling at depth 4 is \(0.9995^4 \approx 0.9980\) while at depth 32 \(0.9995^{32} \approx 0.9841\). One implication of this is that for a given shrink rate, the edge of collapse might appear sharper at deeper networks, but that is to a large extent a consequence of ReLU MLP’s degree \(L\) homogeneity. When controlling for the effective functional gain of a given radius projection, we find that collapse looks more similar across depths.

This depth amplification of radius changes may also help explain why ASMR’s advantage shows some evidence of increasing with depth. As described earlier, under AdamW, the model’s radius is determined indirectly by the equilibrium between weight decay and the optimizer’s updates. ASMR instead explicitly traverses a range of radii. Since progressively smaller radius changes can produce large functional changes as depth increases, this controlled traversal may help ASMR find better solutions within the narrow range near collapse.

Applications

Limited data language models pre-training

The toy problem results are encouraging, but does ASMR’s advantage survive beyond the controlled teacher-student setting? We tackle this question by studying autoregressive language modeling, with a twist. Modern Transformer architectures and their training are a particularly tricky setting for norm control for two reasons. First, related to their architecture, Transformers’ residual streams are heavily normalized, making the Frobenius norm of the embedding matrix $E$ largely irrelevant to prediction. More specifically, because RMSNorm is invariant to uniform rescaling, in an architecture where the input embedding and output head are untied, applying RMSNorm immediately after the embedding lookup makes $E$ and $cE$ (for $c > 0$) produce effectively identical input representations. The embedding radius $ \lVert E \rVert _F $ therefore ceases to be important from a functional point of view: any radial shrinkage is immediately erased by normalization. Thus, we remove normalization from the embedding and the residual stream, while retaining QK norm for training stability, so that the radius controlled by ASMR directly affects the function represented by the model. We nevertheless retain the corresponding normed architecture as a baseline.

Second, related to their training, given the relative abundance of data, language-model pre-training for small and medium sized models typically occurs in the “data-rich” regime, in which each example is seen at most once. This is very different from a conventional multi-epoch training routine. In this data-rich setting, Aitchison (2024) argues that maximum-likelihood training effectively optimizes the population loss (the expected test loss objective), so Bayesian inference (and we can similarly extend, regularization) should offer no advantage specifically in terms of overfitting or calibration. This is consistent with Komatsuzaki (2019), no overfitting and no benefit from conventional regularization in one-epoch Transformer language model training. Weight decay can still affect optimization and the effective learning rate, but its typical role in reducing overfitting diminishes. To study ASMR in a setting where that role is meaningful, we then deliberately move into a data-constrained, multi-epoch setting by training for 100 epochs on a fixed five million token corpus. This restores the possibility of memorization and overfitting, and gives explicit regularization a meaningful generalization problem to solve. We can see this very clearly with the unnormed, unregularized baseline Transformer training run.

Getting into specifics, we base our model on a simplified version of nanochat’s architecture: multi-head self-attention with rotary positional embeddings and QK norm, ReLU² feed-forward network, untied input-embedding and output-projection matrices, and no biases in its linear layers. All experiments were run locally on a 48gb MacBook Pro with an M4 Pro chip, so we look at a small problem and provide as many seeds as were feasible. We use a four-layer, width-256 model with four attention heads, a 4x MLP expansion, and a context length of 512 tokens. Crucially, for everything but the normed baseline, we remove the RMSNorm operations normally applied after the token embedding, within each Transformer block and before the output head. We focus on regularization over the input-embedding matrix only; extending ASMR to other parameter groups is a potentially interesting direction for future work. The five models we compare are a baseline normed one, and four unnormed ones: unregularized, embedding regularized with weight decay, ASMR and a fixed radius max-norm control.

We tune the embedding weight-decay coefficient on the validation set of a seed over $\lambda \in \{0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0\}$ and select $\lambda = 0.5$. Similarly, we pick ASMR’s shrink rate between $\{0.9995, 0.9993\}$ and find $0.9993$ as the best one, reaching a final embedding norm of around 200 (as compared to the unregularized norm of approximately 19,600). We then train on five different seeds; every model is trained for 100 epochs over the fixed five million token corpus. We keep nanochat’s 40 step linear learning rate warmup, after which we apply ASMR’s radius shrinkage or activate the fixed-radius constraint. Table 8 below shows the results.

Limitations and open questions

A continuation method for regularization like ASMR appears to provide a practical advantage over standard weight decay. In the toy problem, ASMR matched AdamW in shallow networks, outperformed it as depth increased, failed to train less often, and exhibited substantially lower sensitivity over the tested hyperparameter grids. The language model experiment extended this pattern to a real-world data setting, where ASMR outperformed every alternative on all five seeds. These experiments, however, show what the methods reach under finite (and low) compute and finite hyperparameter searches, and it remains unclear whether these solutions are provably inaccessible to weight decay, which might reach them with a deeper search. What the experiments do establish, more narrowly, is that ASMR reached these solutions more reliably under the tested budgets. The fixed-radius max-norm controls also show that knowing the optimal norm by itself is not sufficient. Separating the effects of projection, gradual shrinkage and explicit control of the norm trajectory remains an important open question.

The edge of collapse phenomenon is also very interesting and worth further research. Why does the best solution appear in such a narrow region, immediately before the model becomes completely unable to fit the data? This appeared consistently and very clearly in plain MLPs, though in residual networks - like the transformer architecture used above - it wasn’t as clear. The latter seem to accommodate a wider range of possible solutions with wildly different norms and where test losses are essentially the same.

A related limitation of the current approach is the collapse-and-rewind controller used in the toy problem. The original idea was to let the model endogenously balance between continuing to shrink and temporarily stopping or slowing down (or even regrow). Considerable effort went into finding the correct “canary in the coal mine”, but no signal predicted collapse reliably without also stopping shrinkage too early. Finding a genuinely endogenous signal that can stop shrinkage right at the edge of collapse, but before it, and that generalizes across architectures, would make ASMR both cleaner and easier to deploy.

Finally, further research is needed on how ASMR interacts with normalization. Normalized architectures are currently pervasive in the literature, and for good reasons. If overfitting is not an issue, or if normalization makes the chosen parameter norm functionally irrelevant, then ASMR has no clear role as a regularizer. ASMR’s current scope is limited to problems where data is scarce, norm is functionally relevant, and training over a high number of epochs is feasible; which, in today’s contemporary literature, is a very narrow scope. For ASMR to act as a meaningful regularizer in language model pre-training, we had to deliberately use a norm-free Transformer trained repeatedly on a limited corpus. The toy problem suggests that ASMR’s advantage over weight decay may grow with network depth, but whether this extends to larger models remains to be seen. Identifying functionally meaningful parameter groups in normalized architectures, extending ASMR beyond the embeddings, and testing whether its advantage grows with model scale are important empirical directions left open.

Related Work

Norm constraints and scheduled radii. ASMR is not the first method to optimize through a sequence of norm constraints. Most directly, Bietti et al. (2019) give a “stochastic projected gradient with continuation” algorithm whose layer-wise spectral-norm bounds decrease exponentially from a looser constraint to a prescribed terminal value. Liu et al. (2023) optimize on fixed total-weight-norm spheres to study and accelerate grokking. Loshchilov (2023) generalizes decoupled weight decay into Weight Norm Control, in which a target norm may be fixed or scheduled, while Franke et al. (2024) enforce per-matrix norm bounds through an augmented-Lagrangian method. These works rule out a broad novelty claim for shrinking or controlling weight norms. The narrower distinction here is the training regime: a global Frobenius-radius schedule in a scale-sensitive network, no terminal radius chosen in advance, collapse detected from representation SD, and an empirical comparison of the resulting path with fixed-radius and weight-decay paths at similar final norms.

Continuation and graduated optimization. Continuation methods solve a sequence of gradually harder problems, warm-starting each one from the preceding solution. Graduated optimization often implements this idea by progressively removing smoothing from a non-convex objective . ASMR instead leaves the empirical loss unchanged and tightens its feasible set. It is therefore best understood as parameter or constraint continuation rather than as a new projected-gradient method. The contribution pursued in this work is empirical: whether traversing the norm path changes which solution is reached under a finite training budget.

Normalization and hyperspherical optimization. Several adjacent methods hold norms fixed rather than anneal them. Weight Normalization reparameterizes each weight vector into a direction and magnitude ; nGPT normalizes embedding, attention, MLP and hidden-state vectors to the unit sphere ; Target Variance Rescaling periodically rescales weights to control their variance ; and Hyperball fixes matrix Frobenius norms while also normalizing optimizer updates . Work on scale-invariant networks further shows that spherical optimization exposes an effective angular learning rate and can enter convergence, chaotic-equilibrium or divergence regimes . ASMR retains the standard parameterization, does not normalize updates, and deliberately changes a functionally meaningful radius. These differences are central: in a scale-invariant network, radial shrinkage may change optimization dynamics without changing the represented function, whereas the unnormalized networks in this work can collapse as their radius shrinks.

A different “edge” from the Edge of Stability. The name also invites comparison with the Edge of Stability literature. There, full-batch gradient descent progressively sharpens until the largest Hessian eigenvalue hovers near the discrete-time stability threshold \(2/\eta\), with short-run loss oscillations but long-run descent . The edge studied here is not defined by Hessian sharpness or a step-size stability threshold. It is induced by an externally tightening norm constraint: representation and output scale eventually become too small to fit the data. The two phenomena share the broader idea that useful optimization can occur close to a failure boundary, but the boundary variables and observed failure modes are different.

Conclusion

This project started with a very simple observation: if a model’s norm is what mostly explains the double descent behavior in over-parameterized models, why don’t we more directly control it throughout training, as opposed to indirectly targeting it through weight decay? We then implemented a solution as simple as the question itself, using projected gradient descent with a time-varying feasible set, where after each step we project the current iterate to a slightly tighter weight-norm ball. The answer appears to be more interesting than simply obtaining lower-norm models.

In a teacher-student toy problem, ASMR matches tuned conventional regularizers in shallow networks, outperforms them as networks become deeper and the optimization problem becomes harder, is substantially less sensitive to hyperparameter choice and fails to train at a significantly lower rate. Moreover, we show that fixed-radius max-norm regularization, even when given the radius of the best solution that ASMR found, fails to match ASMR’s performance in shallow networks, and is completely unable to train in deeper ones. In a data-limited small language modeling problem, ASMR over the embeddings also consistently outperformed tuned embedding weight decay, the unregularized model, the normed baseline, and the max-norm optimizer provided with ASMR’s validation-best radius.

Crucially, ASMR outperforms alternative regularizers even when they both reach solutions at equal final norms, suggesting that the path an optimizer takes towards a low-norm solution is important. ASMR treats regularization as a continuation path: it begins at the initialization norm, where the model can train healthily, and then progressively asks it to solve the same problem under a slightly tighter constraint. Many questions remain open, but, taken together, these results suggest that the optimization path to a low-norm solution appears to matter independently of the final norm itself.
